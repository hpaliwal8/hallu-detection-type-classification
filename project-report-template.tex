\begin{abstract}
We present a comparative study of hallucination behavior in four open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs (Phi-4-mini, Mistral-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B) on HotpotQA and TruthfulQA under three prompt variants. Our contribution is a reproducible labeling pipeline that combines a DeBERTa-MNLI judge with score-based heuristics to assign each answer one of seven taxonomy labels (\lbl{supported}, \lbl{abstained}, \lbl{contradiction to evidence}, \lbl{entity error}, \lbl{attribute error}, \lbl{multi-hop reasoning error}, \lbl{unsupported inference}). A multi-signal supported-override rescues verbose-but-correct answers, and a cross-record pass flags \lbl{overconfident abstention failure}. Across 7{,}440 labeled records, we find that three of four models hallucinate at near-identical aggregate rates (87--92\%) on HotpotQA plain, while Llama-3.1 stands out at 40\%. Prompt sensitivity is sharply non-uniform: the same reasoning prompt reduces Qwen/Mistral hallucinations by roughly 17 points each but increases Llama-3.1's by 17.8 points. Our type-level analysis suggests that reasoning prompts may shift errors away from direct contradictions and toward attribute-like mistakes, especially numerical mismatches, although manual validation shows that these fine-grained type distributions should be interpreted directionally rather than as exact measurements. On TruthfulQA, all four models converge near 83\% hallucination under reasoning, suggesting misconception-style questions defeat prompt-based mitigation more uniformly than multi-hop factoid questions. Manual validation on a stratified 100-record sample indicates that the binary hallucination rate is well-calibrated, while the fine-grained taxonomy remains noisy. We argue that LLM evaluation should report both aggregate hallucination rates and failure-type distributions, but should explicitly separate robust binary conclusions from more tentative type-level interpretations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Project Overview}

\paragraph{} Large language models are now deployed for QA tasks where factual accuracy directly affects users. Despite their fluency, instruction-tuned LLMs continue to produce confident but factually incorrect outputs, a behavior known as \textit{hallucination}. The problem is hard because hallucinations are coherent, plausible-but-wrong statements that surface fluency checks cannot detect. The model has no internal signal distinguishing what it knows from what it can fluently confabulate. We narrow this problem to: \textbf{given a question, a model answer, and human-annotated supporting facts, automatically classify whether the answer is hallucinated and what kind of hallucination it is}, producing one of seven taxonomy labels per record.

\paragraph{} Prior work falls into three families. \textit{Model-internal} methods such as SelfCheckGPT require white-box logits or multiple samples. \textit{Retrieval-grounded} pipelines such as FactScore decompose answers into atomic claims and verify each. \textit{NLI-based} judges compare a hypothesis, the answer, to a premise, the gold passage. All produce a binary or graded verdict, but fewer yield a fine-grained taxonomy of \textit{why} a hallucination occurred, and fewer apply a uniform pipeline across multiple LLMs. The gap we address is \textbf{a single, reproducible pipeline that classifies hallucinations into an interpretable taxonomy and applies uniformly across models, datasets, and prompts.}

\paragraph{} Our pipeline combines DeBERTa-MNLI with score-based heuristics. The NLI judge gives the supported/hallucinated decision and contributes the contradiction-probability score. For finer typing, three additional scores compete: a NER-based entity score, a number-mismatch score gated on entity-correctness, and an evidence-engagement score gated on question type. The argmax type wins; below a 0.5 confidence floor we return \lbl{unsupported inference}. Two safety nets handle NLI's known weaknesses: a multi-signal supported-override, using token recall, embedding cosine, and bidirectional NLI, for verbose-but-correct answers, and a cross-record second pass that flags overconfident-abstention failures by comparing plain and abstain prompt variants for the same question.

\paragraph{} We apply this pipeline uniformly to four open-weight LLMs (Phi-4-mini-instruct 3.8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) on 500 HotpotQA + 120 TruthfulQA questions $\times$ three prompts (\lbl{plain}, \lbl{abstain}, \lbl{reasoning}), totaling 7{,}440 labeled records. Primary metrics are EM, token-F1, hallucination rate, abstention rate, and the type distribution within hallucinated records.

\paragraph{} Three headline findings motivate the rest of the report. First, on HotpotQA \lbl{plain}, three of four models hallucinate at 87--92\%, while Llama-3.1 stands out at 40\%. Second, the same \lbl{reasoning} prompt reduces Mistral's and Qwen's hallucination rates by roughly 17 points each but \emph{increases} Llama-3.1's by 17.8 points. Third, our pipeline suggests that error \emph{types} shift under reasoning prompts: \lbl{contradiction to evidence} decreases while \lbl{attribute error} increases, although manual validation shows this type-level result should be interpreted as directional rather than exact. On TruthfulQA, all four models converge near 83\% under reasoning, suggesting that misconception-style questions are less responsive to prompt-level mitigation.

\section{Ideas}

\subsection{Idea 1: NLI-Based Evidence-Grounded Labeling}

HotpotQA includes human-annotated supporting facts, which are sentence-level pointers to gold passages. This makes the verification problem a natural NLI task: does the premise, which consists of the gold evidence, entail the hypothesis, which consists of the model answer? We use \texttt{microsoft/deberta-large-mnli} as a frozen judge. DeBERTa's strong MNLI performance motivated its use as the frozen NLI judge, although MNLI accuracy does not directly measure performance on our hallucination-labeling task. Choosing a frozen NLI judge sidesteps both white-box requirements and LLM-as-judge bias, and lets the same judge be applied uniformly across all four models under test.

The main challenge is that NLI mislabels long correct answers as \lbl{neutral}: verbosity dilutes the entailment signal. We address this by (i) constraining the premise to gold supporting sentences only, never distractors, (ii) cleaning prompt prefixes and chat-template role markers from answers before NLI, because otherwise the answer field contains the original prompt and ``\texttt{assistant\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}'' tokens, and (iii) layering the multi-signal override of Idea~3 on top.

\subsection{Idea 2: Score-Based Hallucination Type Classification}

Sequential heuristic chains fail on this task: whichever check fires first wins, even if a later check has stronger signal. Instead, each candidate type produces a score in $[0, 1]$ from independent signals, and we pick the argmax. Below a 0.5 confidence floor, we deliberately return \lbl{unsupported inference} rather than force a wrong type.

Each score is gated on a precondition that filters obvious false positives. \textit{Entity} score uses spaCy NER (PERSON/ORG/GPE/etc.) rather than capitalized-word matching, restricting to actual named entities. \textit{Attribute} score is gated on entity-correctness: number mismatches only count in answer sentences containing a confirmed-correct entity, so wrong-entity answers fall through to \lbl{entity error} instead. \textit{Multi-hop} score requires the answer to overlap at least 0.2 Jaccard with at least one evidence sentence. A model that invented an answer entirely is \lbl{unsupported inference}, not multi-hop. Before this redesign, \lbl{multi-hop reasoning error} had an estimated 80\% false-positive rate. After the gates and confidence floor, the type distribution became more interpretable than the original cascade, though manual validation still shows that individual fine-grained type assignments remain noisy.

\subsection{Idea 3: Multi-Signal Supported Override and Cross-Record Detection}

The supported-override addresses NLI's verbose-correct failure mode. Three independent signals with uncorrelated failure modes compete: \textit{reference recall}, which measures the fraction of reference tokens in the answer and is verbosity-tolerant; \textit{embedding cosine}, using sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 between answer and reference to capture paraphrase; and \textit{bidirectional NLI}, where the answer entails the reference \textit{and} vice versa. Two of three must agree before flipping a record from hallucinated to supported. For \lbl{neutral}-NLI cases, reference recall above 0.5 alone suffices; for \lbl{contradiction}, we require the full 2-of-3 to avoid suppressing real errors. Thresholds (recall 0.50, cosine 0.60) were tuned on roughly 1500 spot-checked records, biased toward false-negatives so we never artificially lower hallucination rates.

The \lbl{overconfident abstention failure} flag cannot be set per-record, because it requires comparing a model's behavior on the same question across two prompt variants. We implement it as a cross-record second pass in \texttt{compute\_metrics.py}: group records by (model, question), and if \lbl{plain} is hallucinated and \lbl{abstain} is correctly abstained, flag the \lbl{plain} record. Isolating this in a separate pass keeps the per-record labeler stateless and the cross-record logic easy to audit.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Models}

Four open-weight instruction-tuned transformers are run closed-book, meaning question only and no context, at temperature 0.2, top-$p$ 0.9, and max-new-tokens 256. Inference uses Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} on Colab T4 (4-bit quantized) and A100 (FP16): \textbf{Phi-4-mini-instruct} (3.8B, Microsoft), \textbf{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3} (7B, Mistral AI), \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} (7B, Alibaba), and \textbf{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} (8B, Meta). The four span 3.8--8B and four provider families so observed differences reflect architecture/training rather than scale alone. Labeling additionally uses \texttt{microsoft/deberta-large-mnli} as the NLI judge, \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} for embeddings, and spaCy's \texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm} for NER.

\subsection{Dataset}

\textbf{HotpotQA (primary):} 500 questions sampled from the dev split (distractor config, seed 42), each with a short reference answer and human-annotated supporting facts (sentence pointers into gold passages). Models see only the question; supporting facts are reserved for the labeling pipeline. \textbf{TruthfulQA (secondary):} 120 questions stratified by category; because TruthfulQA lacks supporting facts, the gold reference itself is used as the NLI premise. Test-set total: 500$\times$3 + 120$\times$3 = 1860 records per model, 7440 across all four. Prompt variants are \lbl{plain}, direct QA; \lbl{abstain}, ``If unsure, say I don't know''; and \lbl{reasoning}, step-by-step plus abstention.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\textbf{Exact Match}: $\text{EM}(p, r) = \mathbb{1}[\text{normalize}(p) = \text{normalize}(r)]$, using lowercase, article stripping, and punctuation removal.
\textbf{Token F1}: harmonic mean of token precision and recall: $F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot P \cdot R}{P + R}$.
\textbf{Hallucination rate}: fraction of records where \texttt{hallucination\_type} $\notin$ \{supported, abstained\}.
\textbf{Abstention rate}: fraction labeled \lbl{abstained}.
\textbf{Type distribution}: percent of hallucinated records per taxonomy bucket.
\textbf{Prompt sensitivity}: $\Delta = \text{Hall}\%_{\text{plain}} - \text{Hall}\%_{\text{reasoning}}$.
\textbf{Overconfident abstention failure}: cross-record fraction where \lbl{plain} hallucinates while \lbl{abstain} abstains. We additionally manually validate a 100-record stratified sample to measure the reliability of binary hallucination detection and fine-grained type classification independently (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

Figure~\ref{fig:hall_by_type} reports the type composition of hallucinated answers by model and prompt. \subsection{Headline Findings}

Four findings summarize the results. First, aggregate hallucination rates are model-similar on HotpotQA \lbl{plain} for Phi-4-mini, Mistral-7B, and Qwen2.5-7B, all between 87--92\%, while Llama-3.1 is a clear outlier at 40\%. Second, prompt sensitivity is sharply non-uniform: reasoning helps Mistral and Qwen, marginally helps Phi, and hurts Llama. Third, the pipeline suggests that reasoning prompts change the distribution of failure modes, with fewer direct contradictions and more attribute-like errors, although this type-level claim is less certain than the binary hallucination-rate result. Fourth, misconception-style TruthfulQA questions defeat reasoning prompts more uniformly across models than HotpotQA questions do.

Overall, our strongest quantitative conclusions concern binary hallucination rates and prompt sensitivity. Type-level results are useful for diagnosing possible mechanisms, but because manual validation finds only 47\% exact agreement on fine-grained labels, they should be interpreted as suggestive rather than definitive.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}

We focus on \textbf{Mistral-7B}, the model with the clearest beneficial reasoning-prompt response, a 17-point hallucination-rate drop, and the most balanced abstention behavior. Manual review of roughly 25 hallucinations per type surfaces four recurring failure patterns. The first is \textit{confident substitution}: Mistral plain answers ``The Public Theater'' when asked the original name of Joseph Papp's organization, while the gold answer is New York Shakespeare Festival, the institution's earlier name. The second is \textit{over-elaboration with embedded fabrications}: Mistral reasoning correctly says ``Woolworth Building was built first'' but inserts ``completed in 1913'', while the gold evidence supports 1910--1912. The third is \textit{confused chaining on bridge questions}, where the model correctly identifies an intermediate entity but mis-attributes its second-hop property. The fourth is \textit{hedged abstention on easy facts}, where the model refuses a question whose evidence contains an unambiguous answer, such as D-Day for the Normandy operation.

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{plots/report_type_shifts.png}
\caption{Mistral-7B hallucination type distribution shifts across prompts. The plot suggests that reasoning reduces direct contradiction-like errors and increases attribute-like errors, but the exact proportions should be interpreted cautiously because fine-grained type labels are noisy.}
\label{fig:type_shifts}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{H1: Reasoning prompts may convert contradictions into attribute-like errors via verbosity.} Mistral's pipeline-assigned \lbl{contradiction to evidence} share drops from 48.3\% to 11.4\% under reasoning while \lbl{attribute error} rises from 18.6\% to 57.0\%. A plausible explanation is that step-by-step reasoning improves the high-level structure of the answer but lengthens the response, creating more opportunities for extra dates, counts, or numerical claims to diverge from the evidence. The Woolworth example illustrates this pattern: the model correctly identifies that the Woolworth Building was built first but adds an incorrect completion date. Because our manual validation shows that type labels are noisy, this hypothesis should be treated as a mechanism suggested by the data rather than proven. A direct follow-up would measure generated-token length by prompt and test whether shorter reasoning responses reduce attribute-like errors.

\paragraph{H2: Bridge questions concentrate multi-hop errors.} HotpotQA's \texttt{bridge} questions require both identifying an intermediate entity \textit{and} retrieving a property of it, which gives two distinct failure points. Llama plain answers ``I'm not familiar with Pak Sao'' on a bridge question whose Wing-Chun hop is the bottleneck. The labeled records carry a \texttt{type} field; a short filter would separate bridge vs.\ comparison rates and test whether multi-hop mistakes are concentrated specifically in bridge questions.

\paragraph{H3: Llama-3.1's anomalous prompt sensitivity may reflect instruction-following style rather than knowledge.} Llama hallucinates 40\% on \lbl{plain} but rises to 58\% on \lbl{reasoning}. Plain outputs frequently hedge or refuse, which are caught by \texttt{ABSTAIN\_PHRASES}; reasoning outputs are more likely to be multi-sentence elaborations where new unsupported claims can appear. This suggests that Llama may interpret ``reason step-by-step'' as an instruction to produce a longer explanatory answer rather than a more cautious answer. A stronger test would compare average response length, abstention phrasing, and hallucination rate across prompt variants.

\paragraph{Manual validation.} We hand-labeled a stratified 100-record sample to measure the reliability of the two levels of our labeling pipeline. The sample was stratified across supported answers, abstentions, and hallucination buckets so that we could evaluate both the binary decision and the fine-grained taxonomy. The results separate cleanly. \textbf{Binary hallucination detection}, meaning whether the answer is hallucinated or not, was correct on 99 of 100 records, which gives us confidence that the aggregate hallucination rates reported in Tables~\ref{tab:hall_rate} and \ref{tab:prompt_cross} are trustworthy. \textbf{Fine-grained type classification}, however, was correct on only 47 of 100 records. This means the exact type percentages in Figures~\ref{fig:hall_by_type} and \ref{fig:type_shifts} should not be interpreted as precise measurements.

Three concrete confusion patterns account for most type-level errors. First and most common, the pipeline labels \lbl{attribute error} when the human-preferred label is \lbl{entity error}: cases where the model invents an entirely wrong entity, but our number-mismatch heuristic fires on dates or figures embedded inside the wrong-entity claim. Second, NLI sometimes returns \lbl{contradiction} on answers that did not engage with the evidence at all, where a human would prefer \lbl{entity error} or \lbl{unsupported inference}. Third, our \texttt{ABSTAIN\_PHRASES} list misses some hedging forms such as ``I am unable to confirm'' or ``I don't have access to'', so a small number of abstentions are pushed into hallucination buckets.

The practical implication is that the binary \lbl{Hall\%} numbers should be treated as the primary quantitative result, while the type-distribution figures should be read as directional diagnostic evidence. In particular, the \lbl{attribute error} bucket is likely inflated relative to \lbl{entity error}, and the \lbl{contradiction to evidence} bucket includes some answers that ignored the evidence entirely. A stronger NER+coreference model, an expanded abstention phrase list, and an LLM-based tiebreaker for low-confidence cases are immediate next steps.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{plots/report_overconfident_abstention.png}
\caption{Overconfident abstention failures: questions where the plain prompt hallucinated but the abstain prompt correctly refused. Phi-4-mini and Qwen2.5 show the largest gaps between their plain and abstain behavior.}

\paragraph{Takeaways.} Three points consolidate. First, prompt effects appear to be mediated partly by response length: reasoning prompts may reduce direct contradictions while increasing opportunities for smaller embedded factual mistakes. Second, prompt strategies that work on multi-hop factoid questions such as HotpotQA do not necessarily transfer to misconception-style questions such as TruthfulQA, where all models converge near 83\% hallucination under reasoning. Third, model-specific instruction-following style matters more than model size in determining how prompts shape hallucination behavior. Hallucination-mitigation prompts should therefore be evaluated per model rather than uniformly applied. Because our type-level classifier is noisy, these takeaways rely most strongly on the binary hallucination-rate trends and more cautiously on the fine-grained type distributions.

\section{Learning Outcomes}

Working on this project taught us more than we expected to learn. Going in, we thought the hard part would be running inference across four LLMs and computing comparison metrics. The metrics turned out to be the easy piece. The hard parts were different. They were the assumptions we did not know we were making, the false confidence our early heuristics handed us, and the slow realization that ``build the pipeline'' and ``build a pipeline whose outputs you trust'' are quite different problems.

\paragraph{A single classifier rarely carries a whole pipeline.} Our initial design treated DeBERTa-MNLI as the source of truth. If it said \lbl{neutral}, the answer was hallucinated. If it said \lbl{entailment}, the answer was supported. This felt clean and self-evidently correct. Then we read a few dozen examples and saw DeBERTa labeling obviously correct verbose answers as \lbl{neutral} because the entailment signal had been diluted by elaboration. The fix was not to swap to a ``better'' judge. It was to accept this judge's known weakness and route around it. The multi-signal supported-override of Idea~3 was unplanned at the start of the project. It emerged from manual false-positive review. The broader lesson is that in any labeling pipeline, the most valuable engineering work may not be on the central classifier itself. It is often on the independent rescue paths that fire precisely on the classifier's known failure modes.

\paragraph{The biggest gains often come from places you were not looking.} Halfway through the project we noticed that most cleaned answer fields still contained the original prompt text and chat-template artifacts like the literal string ``\texttt{assistant\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}''. We had been agonizing over which heuristic check should fire first while the inputs to NLI were polluted. Once we wrote \texttt{clean\_outputs.py} and re-ran the pipeline, Mistral's supported count jumped from 119 to 416. That single 297-record improvement was larger than every algorithmic refinement that came after it combined. This is a lesson we had heard often but had not quite internalized: when an end-to-end system underperforms, audit the inputs to each stage before tuning the model in the middle. The unglamorous fixes are frequently the ones with the highest leverage.

\paragraph{Sequential heuristics are dishonest about uncertainty.} Our first \texttt{classify\_hallucination\_type} was a cascade of \texttt{if/elif/else} branches and it gave us numbers that looked respectable until we did manual spot-checks. The first check that matched won, even when later checks had stronger evidence. \lbl{multi-hop reasoning error} had an estimated 80\% false-positive rate because the bridge-question check fired on every bridge question, regardless of whether the model had engaged with the evidence at all. We replaced the cascade with a score competition. Each candidate type produces a score in $[0, 1]$, and a 0.5 confidence floor demotes uncertain cases to \lbl{unsupported inference}. The refactor was small, but it made the resulting type distribution more interpretable than the original cascade. For taxonomies of more than three or four classes, score-based dispatch is more honest than ordered branches. It makes each decision auditable, and it gracefully admits when no signal is strong enough.

\paragraph{Aggregate metrics hide model differences; types and prompts reveal them.} Three of our four models hallucinate at 87--92\% on HotpotQA's plain prompt. On a binary metric they are essentially identical. We could have written ``all four models behave similarly'' and moved on. The type distribution suggested a different story. Phi-4-mini's hallucinations are dominated by \lbl{contradiction to evidence}; Qwen's reasoning-prompt hallucinations are often assigned to \lbl{attribute error}; Llama-3.1 frequently abstains by hedging where the others contradict. These differences are actionable, but they must be interpreted cautiously because manual validation shows the fine-grained type classifier remains noisy. The same is true for prompts. A single ``reasoning'' instruction reduces Qwen's hallucination rate by 16.6 percentage points and \emph{increases} Llama-3.1's by 17.8 points. Prompt strategies cannot be evaluated on a single model and generalized to others. Comparative LLM evaluation has to be done across the full cross-product of models and prompts, not along either dimension in isolation.

\paragraph{Operational lessons leave the deepest marks.} A handful of less glamorous experiences shaped how we approach experiments now. We lost two hours of Mistral generations to an unflushed file buffer when a Colab session died, and \texttt{f.flush()} after every record is now non-negotiable for long runs. We learned to explicitly clear GPU caches between sequentially loaded Hugging Face models on a 40~GB A100. Otherwise the second model's layers silently get offloaded to CPU and inference drags. Seeded sampling matters more than it sounds when datasets get re-prepared. Finally, keeping the labeled JSONL as the single source of truth, with all metrics, plots, and analyses derived from it rather than computed inline, simplified everything downstream when we needed to relabel after the cleaning fix. Most of these are not techniques you can teach in a lecture. They are the kind of things you only really learn by losing two hours of work.

\paragraph{Closing reflection.} The most important shift this project produced in our thinking is about what it means to ``evaluate an LLM.'' We started believing that evaluation is mostly a measurement problem: pick the right metric, run the right benchmark, report the number. We came out believing it is closer to instrumentation engineering. Every metric makes assumptions about what a correct answer looks like. Every judge has failure modes. And the interesting findings live in the disagreements between metrics rather than in any single value. The comparative study we built is not just a comparison of four models. It is an argument for a particular style of comparison, one that takes failure types seriously, treats prompts as part of the model, and resists the temptation to flatten everything into a single rate.
